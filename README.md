# Framework_v1

### A Metabolite-Anchored Multi-Omics Evidence Framework for Prioritizing Candidate Pharmacogenomic Relationships in Coronary Artery Disease

**Framework_v1** is a computational framework designed to systematically prioritize candidate pharmacogenomic relationships by integrating disease genetics, metabolite-associated genetic variation, Mendelian randomization (MR), human pharmacometabolomics, functional variant annotation, and pharmacological knowledge resources. Developed using coronary artery disease (CAD) as a proof-of-concept, the framework uses circulating metabolites as intermediate molecular phenotypes to connect disease-associated genetic architecture with empirically observed drug-associated metabolic perturbations. This metabolite-anchored strategy enables the identification of candidate gene–drug relationships that may not be apparent from conventional pharmacogenomic association studies or canonical drug-target information alone.

The framework integrates CAD genome-wide association study (GWAS) data with metabolite GWAS through Bayesian colocalization to identify metabolic traits sharing genetic signals with CAD. Genome-wide significant, linkage disequilibrium (LD)-clumped genetic instruments for these traits are subsequently evaluated using two-sample MR to estimate metabolite–CAD associations. These estimates are integrated with population-scale pharmacometabolomic data to identify drug–metabolite relationships in which drug-associated metabolic changes oppose the direction of genetically estimated metabolite effects on CAD. This directional opposition serves as a hypothesis-generation criterion rather than evidence that a drug causally reduces CAD risk.

Retained genetic instruments are annotated using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) and assigned to genes, allowing evidence from multiple metabolic traits to converge into gene-centric, drug-category-specific modules. Rather than combining heterogeneous evidence into a single composite score, Framework_v1 preserves genetic, metabolic, functional, and pharmacological evidence dimensions and applies transparent, sequential criteria for candidate prioritization. CPIC, DGIdb, and ChEMBL are incorporated as complementary knowledge resources for benchmarking, established-relationship exclusion, and additional interaction-evidence annotation.

In the CAD application, the framework constructed **19,375 gene–drug-category modules**. Sequential evidence gating identified **2,083 Tier C**, **357 Tier B**, and **18 Tier A** candidate modules. These nested tiers represent distinct hypothesis-generating evidence strata rather than validated causal relationships or clinical prescribing recommendations. External benchmarking and permutation-based analyses were used to characterize the recovery of established relationships and assess enrichment associated with candidate selection.

Post-prioritization analyses incorporated CAD-specific fine-mapping, high-LD regulatory evidence, gene-prioritization resources, and relevant pharmacogenetic literature to distinguish system-level metabolic convergence from direct CAD-locus reinforcement. Representative findings include *CETP*–statin relationships with locus-reinforced CAD evidence, *ALDH1A2*–selective β-blockers as a hypothesis-generating candidate involving reactive-aldehyde and redox biology, and *SLCO1B1*–ACE inhibitors as a complementary literature-supported pharmacogenetic relationship. These examples illustrate different evidence architectures and highlight opportunities for subsequent experimental investigation.

**Framework_v1 is intended as a transparent, reproducible research platform for multi-omics evidence integration and pharmacogenomic hypothesis generation.** Although initially developed for CAD, its modular architecture may be adapted to other cardiovascular and complex diseases when suitable genetic, metabolomic, and pharmacological datasets are available. The repository provides the computational workflow and supporting scripts for evidence integration, statistical analysis, gene–drug module construction, candidate prioritization, and downstream interpretation. The resulting candidate relationships require further functional and clinical validation and should not be interpreted as established drug targets or actionable pharmacogenomic recommendations.
